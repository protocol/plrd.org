Program overview

More than access.
The capacity to choose.

Sovereign AI is a Protocol Labs R&D program helping countries build the technological and institutional capacity to shape their own AI futures.

Bringing together frontier research, open technologies, interdisciplinary expertise, and catalytic capital, the program develops and tests new approaches to AI-enabled governance — from collective intelligence and public decision-making to institutional accountability and public-sector innovation.

Through research, capacity-building, and real-world government partnerships, SovAI aims to translate breakthroughs into practical, scalable solutions that strengthen national sovereignty, institutional effectiveness, and public benefit.

Research openly.Build local capacity.Test in the real world.

01 / The challenge

Why sovereign AI?
Because capability matters.

AI can make government more responsive, better informed, and easier to take part in. The question is who has the capacity to choose how it works.

01

Dependency

When a country rents every model and every service, it gains a tool without building the capability. Its data, suppliers, and choice of model can answer to someone else.

02

Institutional capacity

People learn by choosing, running, and improving AI themselves. Local teams can adapt it to national languages, law, and priorities — and keep that knowledge when a contract ends.

03

Accountability

Public institutions need to test the models they rely on, control access to sensitive information, and retain an audit trail. Human decision-makers remain responsible for the result.

04

Better institutions

Hear more people. Understand trade-offs. Check what a policy actually changed. Sovereign AI is an opportunity to build better governance systems, not simply automate the old ones.

Sovereign still needs testing. Open models can lag closed ones on difficult tasks, and a model built at home can carry its own bias. Ownership does not replace evaluation.

Collective intelligence

Broad Listening
& Simocracy

New approaches to collective intelligence, participation, and public decision-making.

Explore the research: Broad Listening and Simocracy

Listen at scale. Keep people in charge.

Broad Listening reads responses to an open question in people’s own words and languages. A language model extracts claims, maps topics, and writes a report. Each finding links back to the quotes behind it, so participants can check and correct the summary.

Simocracy gives a participant an AI representative — a sim — with a constitution describing their values and red lines. Sims deliberate on proposals; the S-Process translates their judgments into a proposed budget split. People hold the final decision authority.

  1. Ask. One question, with a clear use for the answers.
  2. Listen. Read the answers and check the map against people’s words.
  3. Deliberate. Sims debate proposals, with an inspectable record.
  4. Decide. The person with the mandate approves, changes, or vetoes the proposal.

A research example, not a government deployment: at Funding the Commons in San Francisco in March 2026, sims helped allocate a real budget. Public consultations, citizens’ assemblies, and participatory budgeting are possible government applications.

A sim is not its person.

Models can drift from the constitution their person wrote. These tools widen debate; they do not supply a democratic mandate. People remain accountable for the decision. Open AT Protocol records can make the process inspectable, but sensitive data still needs protection under agreed access rules.

Accountable funding

Hypercerts

New mechanisms for funding, evaluating, and rewarding verified public impact — keeping claims, evidence, and assessments distinct.

Explore the research: Hypercerts

A shared record. Not a blank form.

A hypercert starts with an activity claim: what a project did, where, and when. Evidence, measurements, independent evaluations, and funding can be attached over time. Each signal remains attributed to the person or organization that published it.

Records on the AT Protocol live in accounts projects control. A funder can start from the existing history rather than asking a project to rebuild its application from scratch. Each funder chooses which signals to trust and how to weigh them.

  1. Claim. The project describes the work.
  2. Evidence. It attaches updates and measurements.
  3. Review. Others add their own attributed assessments.
  4. Fund. Funders record support; the next funder can build on the history.

Explore PL R&D’s research-retreat evidence records. Potential government uses include grants, matching funds, payments tied to agreed results, and public reporting — with confidentiality and access rules set for each use.

A record is not proof.

A hypercert records a claim; it does not certify that the claim is true. Trust depends on the evidence and the people evaluating it. A false claim is as easy to publish as a true one. The record does not decide which work is valuable.

For Governments For Funders

For governments

Bring one public problem.

We work as an R&D and implementation partner: building local capacity and deploying open technology around your government’s own public priorities.

What we bring

Technical expertise
Choose, host, and adapt open models in your languages, with your data, on infrastructure your government controls.
Institutional design
Define ownership, access, procurement terms, oversight, and red lines before any data moves.
Evaluation
Test models against local priorities and measure the pilot with real users against the current process.
External resources
Help assemble grants, compute, research partners, and convenings around your team.

From problem to decision

  1. Pick the problemOne public problem, a named owner, and a measure of success agreed at the start.
  2. Set the rulesAgree data rules, oversight, and red lines; then choose the model and where it runs.
  3. Build and testAdapt to local language and data. Compare performance with the way the work is done today.
  4. Decide and hand overScale, change, or stop on the evidence. The code, the know-how, and the team stay with the government.

Proposed success bar — not a completed result. A real public service used by more than 1,000 people over at least 30 days, matching or beating the current process.

What we ask of a partner

  • An owner. A minister or agency lead accountable for the problem.
  • Access. People, data, and systems under rules both sides agree.
  • A decision. Commit to scale, change, or stop at the end.
  • Shared learning. Share lessons within confidentiality so others can build on them.

A consultation, a citizen service, sensitive-document translation, or participatory budgeting can be a starting point.

For funders

Back what countries can build on.

Support research, public-interest infrastructure, and scalable pilots. Funding helps governments see what works for their priorities before committing their own budgets.

The published program aim

3–5 countries. Over 24 months.

An ambition, not a confirmed cohort or a report of completed pilots ↗

What funding pays for

The pilot
An open model adapted to national languages and data, and engineers building the service with the government’s team.
Independent evaluation
Academic partners test the models and compare the pilot with the current process. Report results, good or bad.
A local team
Training and side-by-side work for the officials and engineers who will keep running and improving the system.
Shared lessons
Program coordination, gatherings where teams compare notes, and an open playbook other countries can reuse.

An evidence-led path

  1. Diagnose · Months 0–3Agree on one public task, its owner, and a measure of success.
  2. Design · Months 3–6Set data rules, oversight, red lines, and the technical approach.
  3. Pilot · Months 6–12Test a real service with real users against the baseline. The proposed success bar is more than 1,000 users over at least 30 days.
  4. Decide and hand overScale, change, or stop. Retain the skills and code locally, and share reusable lessons within confidentiality.

Choose where you can help

  • Anchor the program. Support first pilots, shared evaluation, and the program team.
  • Back one country. Fund a pilot in a country or region where you work.
  • Give compute. Offer credits or hosting where the government chooses to run its models.
  • Scale what works. Help a successful pilot become a national service.

Not every pilot will work.

Some pilots will stop at the decision step. One pilot does not make a country self-sufficient in AI; that takes people who stay, a budget line, and years of use. The program funds the first working system and the evidence a government needs to decide what comes next.
